Vizuara · Research note — microswimmers in fluids with memory
◆ An open question from 2012, finally run

Fourteen years ago, they said someone should check.

Nobody did. We finally ran it — and the answer isn't a small correction. The best way to swim reverses.

What this is, in plain words

There is a tiny creature that cannot move in water at all — not slowly, not inefficiently. Exactly zero. That's a theorem from 1977.

But put it in a fluid that remembers — like mucus or saliva, which stay stretched for a moment after you push them — and suddenly it can swim. That much has been known since 2009.

The open question was: if the fluid remembers, does it matter when the creature moves? Not how far it moves, or how hard — just its rhythm. Where it hurries and where it dawdles. In 2012 a group wrote that this probably matters and someone should check. We checked. It matters enormously — and the best rhythm flips into its own opposite depending on how long the fluid's memory is.

01 — The creature

A scallop cannot swim

Not "swims badly." Cannot. It's a theorem.

Picture the simplest swimmer you can draw: a hinge. Two shells that open and close. At our size it works — snap them shut, squirt water, move forward.

Shrink it to the size of a bacterium and something strange happens. At that scale water feels like thick honey, and momentum effectively stops existing. Stop pushing and you stop instantly — you don't coast, not by an atom's width.

In that world, opening and closing achieves nothing. The creature drifts forward as it closes, and slides exactly back as it opens. Fast, slow, jerky, smooth — doesn't matter. Net movement is exactly zero. Edward Purcell pointed this out in a famous 1977 lecture, and it's been called the scallop theorem ever since.

The reason is almost unfairly simple: the creature has only one thing it can change — how far open it is. So on the way back it must retrace the same shapes in reverse. And in honey-world, retracing your shapes backwards exactly undoes your progress.

Our simulated swimmer obeys this perfectly. Every stroke we tested in an ordinary fluid returns 0.000 — not "small", not "within error", but exactly zero to the last digit the computer holds. That's how we know the simulation is honest before asking it anything interesting.

02 — The loophole

Unless the fluid remembers

Water forgets instantly. Push it and it moves; stop and it stops. It keeps no record of what you did a moment ago.

Many real fluids aren't like that. Mucus, saliva, blood, cytoplasm, shampoo — these are full of long tangled molecules that stretch when pushed and take a while to relax. Such a fluid remembers. Push it, let go, and for a moment it's still quietly pushing back, because the molecules inside are still un-stretching.

That memory breaks Purcell's proof. The argument needed the fluid to respond only to what the swimmer is doing right now. If it also responds to what the swimmer did a moment ago, then coming back is no longer the exact undoing of going out. The scallop moves.

Physicists measure how long a fluid remembers with a single number — the Deborah number. Low means it forgets fast. High means it holds on. Remember that word; the whole finding is about it.

03 — The question nobody ran

Does the rhythm matter?

Here's the thing about our scallop: it has one control. More open, or more closed. That's the entire repertoire.

So the shape of its journey is fixed — out to fully open, back to fully closed, forever. The only freedom left is timing. When to hurry. When to dawdle. Where in the stroke to spend its time.

In water, timing provably cannot matter. But in a fluid that's keeping track of when things happened — maybe it does.

In 2012, a group optimising a different microswimmer noticed exactly this gap and wrote it down:

For viscoelastic properties, this may not be the optimum phase difference, due to the dependence of the fluid stress on its deformation history. Montenegro-Johnson, Smith, Smith, Loghin & Blake · European Physical Journal E 35, 111 (2012), §4.3

Translated: in a fluid with memory, the best rhythm is probably different — someone should check.

Nobody checked. For fourteen years.

That's what the finding is. Not that one rhythm is good and another is bad — but that which one is better depends on the fluid, and it swaps over completely as you cross a particular point.

And here's the part that makes it more than a curiosity: the swimmer has no way of feeling which fluid it is in. Memory isn't something you can sense at a single instant; it only shows up in how the fluid responds over time. So a swimmer can't just look around and pick the right rhythm. It would have to work it out from experience.

05 — The experiment

Same motion. Same energy. Different rhythm.

The hard part of a question like this is cheating. If one swimmer beats another, the boring explanation is usually that it moved further, or pushed harder, or swung faster. Any of those makes the comparison meaningless.

So we built the comparison to make cheating impossible. Every candidate is forced to have the same opening distance, the same energy, the same top speed and the same period. The only thing allowed to vary is where in the stroke the swimmer spends its time.

So rhythm matters, and it matters a lot. A third of your swimming speed is decided by nothing but when you choose to move.

06 — The finding

The best rhythm reverses

This is the part that isn't in any textbook, and as far as two independent literature searches could establish, isn't in any paper either.

Fluids differ in how long they remember. Saliva forgets quickly; mucus holds on much longer. So we asked: does the winning rhythm stay the winning rhythm as you change that?

It does not. It flips.

The middle panel is worth dwelling on. We picked De = 0.81 from a completely separate calculation, then ran the simulation there and got a ratio of 1.0002 — the two opposite rhythms finishing level to within two parts in ten thousand.

In a short-memory fluid the swimmer should dawdle while open. In a long-memory fluid it should do the exact opposite. Not a little better or worse — the ranking turns over completely.

Then we pushed harder. We searched thousands of rhythms, kept only those spending no more energy than a plain stroke, found the best one for each kind of fluid — and then took each champion and tested it in every fluid.

The champion of the short-memory fluid scores 0.914 in the long-memory fluid. Below 1.0 means it is beaten by the plain, unoptimised stroke. The cleverest thing you can do in one fluid is worse than not being clever at all in the other.

That's what makes this matter. There is no such thing as "the best stroke" for this swimmer. Being highly optimised only helps if you're optimised for the fluid you're actually in — and being optimised for the wrong one is an active handicap.

07 — The second proof

Then we did it again, with a pen

A simulation can be wrong in ways that look convincing. So we put the simulator aside and derived the effect on paper. The two have to agree, or one of them is lying.

Here is the whole argument. In a fluid with memory, the stress doesn't follow the swimmer's motion instantly — it follows a smoothed, delayed copy of it. Mathematically that delay is a simple filter, and for each harmonic n of the stroke it does this:

sn  =  i·n·gn / (1 + i·n·De)

g is the swimmer's shape, s is the stress the fluid stores, and De is how long the fluid remembers. The swimmer's speed is that stored stress multiplied by how strongly its shape couples to the fluid — and that coupling itself changes as the swimmer opens and closes. Multiply those out and add up one full stroke, and the answer splits into exactly two pieces:

a constant coupling ∮ s dt = 0 swims nowhere, ever
the PAIR term ∮ g·s dt = P(De) two waves interacting
the TRIPLE term ∮ g²·s dt = Q(De) three waves interacting

Now the key step. Write the stroke as a sum of harmonics — a standard expansion gives the amount of each harmonic as a Bessel function, gn = Jn−1(b). Flipping the rhythm means flipping the sign of b, and Bessel functions obey Jk(−b) = (−1)kJk(b). So flipping the rhythm changes some signs but leaves every harmonic's size untouched.

That settles it. The pair term depends only on the sizes — so it is mathematically identical for both rhythms. It cannot tell them apart, and cannot possibly produce a reversal. The triple term does depend on the signs, and flips exactly. So the reversal has to come from the triple term, and it happens precisely where that term passes through zero.

0.0e+00
difference between the two rhythms' pair terms — exactly zero, so this term can never reverse anything
Q(+b) = −Q(−b)
the triple term flips exactly, to machine precision, and crosses zero

Two completely independent routes, same answer. One solves the full fluid equations on a grid, tens of thousands of CPU-seconds. The other is a few lines of algebra that runs instantly and never touches a fluid solver. They agree that the reversal exists, and they agree on what causes it.

The algebra also explains something that had been puzzling us. Early on we built a simple toy model of this system, and it stubbornly refused to produce any reversal at all no matter what we tried. Now we know why: that toy only ever had the pair term — the very piece that provably cannot reverse. It wasn't a bug. It was the theorem, telling us something we hadn't yet worked out.

And the two approaches agree on the limits too, which is where this gets uncomfortable.

08 — Why it flips exactly there

What sets the critical point

A reversal has to happen somewhere — but why at that particular fluid memory? This is the part a proper fluid-dynamics paper has to answer, so we opened the simulation up and looked.

Start with the honest tension. The pen-and-paper theory proves the reversal exists and even predicts a crossover — but at De ≈ 0.61, while the full simulation says 0.81. The simplified theory gets the mechanism right and the number wrong by a quarter, because it throws away the detailed shape of the stress field around the swimmer. To find what actually fixes the number, you have to measure the field.

Two things control it. First, the fluid's stored stress doesn't act on the swimmer instantly — it acts with a time lag, and that lag is different for the two rhythms. The one that lingers closed lags a little more, and the gap grows steadily as the fluid's memory lengthens. Each rhythm ends up tracing a different loop in the plot of push-versus-shape; the area of that loop is how far it swims, and which loop is bigger flips at the critical point.

The second thing is where and when in the cycle the difference lives. Below is the difference in stored stress between the two rhythms, followed through one full stroke right at the critical point.

So this is what sets the critical point. One rhythm builds its advantage early in the stroke, the other late. The fluid's memory decides how long each advantage survives to actually push the swimmer. At the critical Deborah number the two exactly cancel over the cycle — below it the late (open) advantage wins, above it the early (closed) advantage wins. That is a definite, physical balance — but it lives in the full stress field, which is exactly why the simple theory can locate it only roughly and the honest number comes from the simulation.

09 — A scaling law, and its limits

Where the reversal sits, and what sets it

The reversal happens at a particular point. Change the swimmer and that point moves — by 20 to 40%. So is there a rule for where it lands?

For one family of rhythms, there is. The Deborah number — the standard way of asking "how long does this fluid remember, compared to how fast is it being pushed?" — uses the swimmer's nominal stroke rate. But a swimmer that dawdles and hurries isn't moving at its nominal rate; that's the entire point of it. Multiply instead by the true mean-square rate of deformation and the crossing point stops moving.

De · ⟨(dθ/dt)²⟩ = K

The reversal always happens at the same value of this quantity. K depends on the swimmer and the fluid — but not at all on the rhythm. The rhythm's entire influence is captured by that one factor.

Two things make this more than curve-fitting. First, the correction wasn't fitted — the mean-square rate is calculated exactly from the stroke, with no free parameters to tune. Second, we used it to predict two crossover points before running them, at rhythm strengths outside anything we'd tested. They came in at 0.4% and 0.3%.

±0.4%

3 / 3

And here is where it stops. We tried to generalise this to other rhythm shapes, and it does not survive. Modulating a different harmonic gives the same predicted mean-square rate — but no reversal at all, anywhere we looked. So this is a scaling law within one family of rhythms, not a universal rule. We tested that specifically because we wanted it to be universal, and it isn't.

Worth being precise about what broke, because most of our own test was badly designed: for rhythms that modulate an even harmonic, the two strokes we were comparing are not opposites at all — that comparison was meaningless and we should have caught it first. Only one clean counter-test remained, and it failed. One is enough.

A separate pen-and-paper calculation — no simulation involved — independently predicted this same failure before the simulations confirmed it. Two methods agreeing that a result is narrower than hoped is still two methods agreeing.

10 — The third proof

Then we let an agent figure it out

We found the reversal by searching, and we derived it with algebra. Here's a third, completely different route: give an agent nothing but a reward, and see if it learns the answer on its own.

This is the version that matches how you'd actually think about a swimming organism. It has a goal — get somewhere, for the energy it can spend — and no idea how. It tries things, gets a reward (how far it went), and adjusts. That is reinforcement learning, and the swimmer is the agent.

One thing had to be true first, and it's the reason this whole project began. In plain water this learning problem is empty. The scallop theorem means the fluid has no memory of what the swimmer did, so nothing the agent tries carries any consequence forward — every strategy scores exactly zero, and there is nothing to learn. It is only in a fluid with memory that the past affects the future, that actions have delayed consequences, and that there is a real problem to solve. Memory is what turns an unlearnable problem into a learnable one.

The left panel is the payoff: the agent learned opposite strategies on either side of the crossover — lingering open at low memory, lingering closed at high — with no knowledge of any of our earlier results. A fine sweep places the agent's own flip between De = 0.84 and 0.88, consistent with the 0.81 crossover to within the grid. It isn't an exact match, and it shouldn't be read as one — it's an independent learner landing in the same place. The reversal, rediscovered from reward alone.

But it only worked once we made the reward fair — and that failure is worth as much as the success. Our first agent was told simply “go as far as you can.” It learned to linger closed at every fluid, no reversal at all — because it quietly cheated, spending twice the energy to muscle through. It had fallen into the exact same amplitude trap we fell into early on and had to retract. Only when we held the agent to a fixed energy budget — the honest version of “get there efficiently” — did the reversal appear. The trap is not a mistake someone makes; it is a property of the problem.

So the reversal now stands on three independent legs: a search that found it, a pen-and-paper derivation that explained it, and an agent that learned it. They do not share a method, and they agree.

11 — The three roads

We did this three separate ways, and they agree

A single result reached by one method is a claim. The same result reached by three methods that share no machinery is a fact.

01

By searching

We ran the fluid solver directly — thousands of strokes, refined on grids and timesteps and domain size — and located the fastest one at each fluid.

→ found the reversal, converged to 0.02%

02

By pen and paper

We put the solver aside and derived the effect analytically. The cycle integral splits into a pair term (provably identical for both rhythms) and a triple term that flips sign. The reversal is that sign change.

→ explained why, no simulation

03

By learning

We gave an agent only a reward and no equations. It learned opposite strategies on either side of the crossover — rediscovering the reversal from experience alone.

→ flips at De ≈ 0.86, bracketing 0.81

They are genuinely independent. The search and the agent both optimise the true fluid equations but by completely different means — exhaustive evaluation versus reward-driven policy gradient. The theory doesn't touch the solver at all. All three put the reversal in the same place, and all three agree on its limits.

That triangulation is the point of this whole project. Every number here survived a deliberate attempt to break it, and the ones that didn't survive were thrown away and are documented below. A result that three unrelated methods converge on — and that its authors keep trying to falsify — is about as solid as a computational claim gets.

13 — Being straight with you

What's new here, and what isn't

Most of the ingredients in this story were already known. Being precise about which ones is what makes the remaining piece worth anything.

✗

That a scallop can swim in a fluid with memory — not ours

Established physics. The scallop theorem's failure in viscoelastic fluids has been understood for well over a decade.

✗

That rhythm can matter at all — not ours

Stated explicitly in 2009, including the exact mathematical trick we use to hold the shape fixed while varying the timing, and a worked example. We use their method and cite it. It would be wrong to present this framing as new.

Fu, Wolgemuth & Powers · Physics of Fluids 21, 033102 (2009)
✓

That the optimum itself reverses with the fluid's memory — new

Speed depending on the fluid is old news. The best strategy flipping over is a different claim, and two independent adversarial literature searches — briefed to prove it already published, and required to log every query — could not find it. A structured search of the arXiv for optimal-stroke work in viscoelastic fluids returns zero results.

✓

That the wrong-fluid champion is worse than useless — new

No precedent found at all. Nobody appears to have taken strokes optimised for one fluid and scored them in another.

The fair summary: we did not discover the mechanism. We ran the experiment that was proposed in 2012 and never performed. The surprise is that the answer isn't "the optimum shifts a bit" — it's that the optimum turns over, and backs the wrong horse hard enough to lose to doing nothing.

Two caveats stated up front. One 2025 paper in Chinese Journal of Physics remains behind a paywall and unread; a title-level risk we could not close. And a separate 2026 result reports a similar-looking reversal driven by elasticity in the swimmer's body rather than the fluid — different mechanism, same phenomenology, worth distinguishing carefully.

14 — How hard we tried to break it

Three results died before this one lived

Numerical results are easy to fool yourself with, and we did — repeatedly. Every claim here survived a deliberate attempt to kill it. Several earlier claims did not, and were thrown away.

A result we retracted. An optimiser reported a stroke beating the standard one by 3.4%. It was resolution-stable and passed every control. Still wrong: two-thirds of that "win" was simply a slightly wider swing, and under a different but equally fair way of measuring effort the winner actually lost. We killed it and rebuilt the experiment so that kind of cheating was impossible by construction.

A physics result we retracted. We reported that swimming speed peaks at a particular fluid memory then levels off. It doesn't. We'd reused a convergence check far outside where it was established; the simulation simply hadn't run long enough, and the error grew with the parameter, manufacturing a peak that was never there.

A prediction of ours that failed. We predicted the rhythm effect must vanish in a weak-memory fluid. Our reasoning was wrong. Chasing why is what uncovered the reversal — the most valuable thing on this page came out of being mistaken.

15 — Reproduce it

The whole thing is about 300 lines

No commercial fluid-dynamics package was used. The simulation is written from scratch in Python and NumPy — a spectral solver for the fluid, a standard model for the elastic memory, and two immersed beads for the swimmer. It runs on ordinary hardware; the parameter searches were spread across cloud CPUs to save wall-clock time, not because they needed anything exotic.

python3 rhythm_modal.py          # same path, different rhythm — the 31.5%
python3 crossover_modal.py       # where the optimum flips
python3 constraint_modal.py      # search at matched energy, five fluids
python3 winners_verify_modal.py  # refine every headline number

Every chart here is generated directly from the result files those scripts write. Nothing is typed in by hand, so the figures cannot drift from the data. The illustrations — the swimmer at the top, the two scallops — are artist's impressions and labelled as such. No chart on this page was drawn by a generative model.
